Authentication lives under **Configuration › Authentication** in the admin console. It covers how people sign in (OIDC or SAML) and how an identity provider's groups map onto project membership (SCIM).

## Configure an identity provider

<Steps>
  <Step title="Open Configuration › Authentication">
    The list shows any providers already configured, one live per protocol.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Add a provider">
    Choose **OpenID Connect** or **SAML 2.0**.

    <Tabs>
      <Tab title="OpenID Connect">
        Issuer URL (a `.well-known/openid-configuration` URL is accepted and reduced to its issuer), Client ID, client secret, redirect URI, scopes, and whether e-mail verification is required.
      </Tab>
      <Tab title="SAML 2.0">
        Identity-provider entity ID, SSO URL, single-logout URL, and certificate(s); this service's own entity ID and ACS URL; name-ID format; the e-mail and name attribute mappings; whether to sign AuthnRequests; the service provider certificate; and clock-skew tolerance.

        **NameID format** is a select. The values are *Not requested* (the default), *Unspecified*, *Email address*, *Persistent* and *Transient*. With *Not requested*, the identity provider sends its own default format. With *Transient*, the NameID changes at each sign-in, so also set the e-mail attribute.

        The service provider metadata is at `https://<host>/auth/saml/metadata`. Import it at the identity provider after you enable the provider. It is available with or without **Sign authentication requests**. It always contains the entity ID, the ACS URL (HTTP-POST) and the selected NameID format, and it asks for signed assertions. It contains the service provider certificate only when requests are signed.
      </Tab>
    </Tabs>
  </Step>
  <Step title="Decide whether it may sign in SCIM-provisioned users">
    **Users provisioned by SCIM may sign in through this provider** is off by default. Turn it on only for the provider paired with your SCIM client (for example, Entra ID's sign-in app when Entra also provisions users). While it is off, a first sign-in through this provider cannot take over an account that SCIM created; the sign-in is refused as a conflict. A provider configured through environment variables instead of this screen uses `OIDC_ADOPT_SCIM_USERS=true` for the same setting.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Enable it for logins">
    Turning a provider on retires any other provider of the same protocol — only one live OIDC and one live SAML definition can exist at a time, because each protocol is served on one route.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Each account is bound to its identity provider

An account that signs in through OIDC or SAML is bound to three values: the provider key, the issuer the provider trusts, and the subject (the OIDC `sub` or the SAML NameID). The issuer is the OIDC **Issuer URL** or the SAML **Identity-provider entity ID**. A provider configured through environment variables has the key `@environment` and the issuer `OIDC_ISSUER_URL`. A sign-in uses an account only when all three values match. If the same subject comes from two providers, each provider has its own binding.

So a provider whose issuer changed cannot sign in to an account that was linked under the old issuer. The sign-in gets `409` with the cause `provider_not_bound`, and no session is made. This applies to the web, the desktop app and the mobile app. The desktop and mobile apps sign in through the same OIDC and SAML callbacks in the system browser.

### Who may change trust

Only a super admin may make these changes. Another administrator gets `403` with a stable `code`, the screen says why, and the Audit Trail records the refusal:

| Change | `code` | Audit Trail action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Create or delete an identity provider. Turn it on or off. Replace or clear its secret. Change its issuer, client ID, redirect URI or Entra settings (OIDC), or its entity ID, SSO URL, NameID format, e-mail attribute, service provider entity ID, ACS URL, request signing or service provider certificate (SAML). Add a certificate or an Entra tenant, raise the clock-skew tolerance, turn on **Users provisioned by SCIM may sign in through this provider**, or turn off e-mail verification. | `identity_provider_trust_super_admin_required` | `identity_provider.trust_change_refused` |
| Re-bind accounts to a provider | `identity_binding_super_admin_required` | `identity_binding.rebind_refused` |
| Create, rotate or delete a SCIM client | `scim_client_super_admin_required` | `scim_client.change_refused` |

A provider refusal also lists the changed trust fields in `fields`. Other edits stay open to any administrator with the Authentication permission: the display name, the scopes, the login domains, the name attribute, and changes that only narrow trust (removing a certificate or a tenant, lowering the clock skew, turning on e-mail verification). A save made against a provider that changed after the form was opened gets `409`; reload the provider and save again. A change that a super admin makes is recorded as `identity_provider.trust_changed`, `scim_client.created`, `scim_client.rotated` or `scim_client.deleted`.

### Who can change what

Identity setup is split by action into a trust half and a settings half. Each half has its own permission, and the permissions the routes accepted before still work.

| Action | Accepted permissions |
| --- | --- |
| **Read**: the identity provider list, the SCIM client list, the SCIM group bindings and the roles of a project | `identity.sso.view`, or the permission the route accepted before: `runtime.plugins` (providers), `admin.auth.users` (SCIM clients), `configuration.roles.user_project_permissions.view` (bindings) |
| **Trust**: every change in the table above, deleting an identity provider, and re-binding accounts | `identity.sso.trust.manage` or `admin.auth.users.super_admin`. Both belong to the `super_admin` role only. No other permission opens a trust action |
| **Settings**: the display name, the scopes, the login domains, the name attribute, and the changes that only narrow trust | `identity.sso.settings.manage` or `runtime.plugins` |
| **Settings**: revoke a SCIM client | `identity.sso.settings.manage` or `admin.auth.users` |
| **Settings**: create, change or delete a SCIM group binding | `identity.sso.settings.manage` or `configuration.roles.user_project_permissions.edit` |

A save of an identity provider is classified by comparing the stored provider with the body. If the body changes any trust field, the whole save is a trust action. A save to a key that does not exist yet creates a provider, so it is a trust action. A field added to the provider in a later release counts as a trust field until it is listed as a setting.

A group binding also needs the role it grants. You must hold every permission of that role in that project, or the save gets `403` with the code `role_permission_not_held`. Holding `identity.sso.settings.manage` does not change this. In practice, a binding to a project role is written by a super admin or by a member of the project who holds that role.

The permissions `identity.sso.view` and `identity.sso.settings.manage` can be given to a narrower role on the **Roles** page. `identity.sso.trust.manage` cannot: the platform refuses it on every role except `super_admin`.

### Rotate an issuer: re-bind the accounts

When your identity provider really moved to a new issuer (a new tenant, a new Keycloak realm, a new ADFS entity ID), a super admin first saves the provider with the new issuer. The save answers with `previous_issuer`, and the Authentication screen shows a warning that the existing accounts are still bound to it. Then the super admin re-binds the accounts of the old issuer to the new one:

```bash
curl -X POST "https://<host>/api/v2/admin/identity_providers/administration/corporate/rebind" \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $SUPER_ADMIN_TOKEN" -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"from_issuer": "https://old-idp.example.com/realms/corp"}'
```

`$SUPER_ADMIN_TOKEN` is a personal access token of the super admin with `admin_access` `inherit`: a new token carries no administration rights (see [Which credential carries which administration rights](#which-credential-carries-which-administration-rights)). The answer is `{"provider_key": "corporate", "issuer": "<new issuer>", "rebound": <count>}`. The old binding is removed, so the old issuer stops working. A repeated request moves nothing.

- To move the accounts of another provider, add `"from_provider_key": "<key>"`. Use `"from_provider_key": "@environment"` for the accounts of a provider that was configured through environment variables, when you replace it with a provider on this screen.
- To bind accounts that have no binding, send `{"from_unbound": true}` alone. After the upgrade, these are the accounts of a protocol that had no enabled stored provider. An account linked by a replica of the previous release while the upgrade rolled out is bound at its next sign-in, as long as the provider has not been saved since the upgrade.

Each re-bind writes the security audit event `ai.elitea.security.v1.identity.link.changed` with the action `identity.binding.rebound`, and the Audit Trail records `identity_binding.rebound`.

<Warning>
Re-bind only when you are sure that the new issuer is the same directory as the old one. A re-bind lets the new issuer sign in to every account it moves. The platform does not re-link accounts by e-mail address, because the address is a claim of the new identity provider.
</Warning>

## The first global administrator

`identity.initial_global_admins` (or `ELITEA_INITIAL_GLOBAL_ADMINS` on a deployment without an authentication document) names the accounts that get `super_admin` at their first sign-in. It is a **bootstrap only**: it applies only while no active account holds `super_admin`.

- While nobody holds `super_admin`, the first listed account to sign in gets it. A suspended `super_admin` does not count, so the list can still recover a deployment whose only global administrator is suspended.
- Once an active account holds `super_admin`, a listed account that signs in gets no role. The server logs a warning that says which kind of entry matched (`reference` or `email`), but not the address, and how to add an administrator instead.
- Add later administrators from the console of an existing administrator (or with `POST /api/v2/admin/auth_users/administration`). Then empty the list: anyone who can edit it and owns an account at your identity provider could otherwise become global administrator while no `super_admin` is active.
- A deployment with no administrator left, or one where the list was never set, gets one with the operator command [`elitea-main admin-role grant`](/deployment/operations#administrative-roles-from-the-command-line), run inside the main workload.

## OIDC sign-in rules

The platform links an account to the OIDC subject (the id_token `sub` claim), stored as `oidc:<sub>`. The email claim only selects the account on the first login of a subject. These rules apply to every OIDC sign-in:

| id_token | Result |
| --- | --- |
| No `email` claim | Refused: 400 `email claim required`. |
| `"email_verified": false` | Refused on every login: 403 `email claim is not verified`. **This is always so.** Neither `OIDC_REQUIRE_EMAIL_VERIFIED` nor the provider's e-mail verification setting changes it. |
| No `email_verified` claim, subject already linked | Accepted. |
| No `email_verified` claim, first login of the subject | Accepted when e-mail verification is not required. Refused with 403 `email claim is not verified` when it is required. |
| `"email_verified": true` | Accepted. |

"E-mail verification is required" is the **whether e-mail verification is required** setting of a provider on this screen. For a provider configured through environment variables, it is `OIDC_REQUIRE_EMAIL_VERIFIED=true`. The default is off, because many identity providers omit the claim.

The platform requests the scopes `openid`, `profile` and `email` (a provider on this screen can set its own list). Keep the `email` scope:

- In **Keycloak**, keep the `email` client scope assigned to the client. If you remove it, Keycloak refuses the authorization request with `invalid_scope`. Without the scope, the id_token also has no `email` claim, and the login is refused.
- In Keycloak, a user whose **Email verified** flag is off gets `"email_verified": false`, so the login is always refused. Turn the flag on for the user, or let the user verify the address in Keycloak.

## When an OIDC login answers 409

A 409 means that the login cannot use the account that the email claim names. Only an operator can resolve it. The body names the cause, and the elitea-main log has the warning `OIDC: refused a login that names two accounts` with the `sub` and the `cause`:

| Body starts with | `cause` in the log | Meaning and remedy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `this identity is already linked to another account. If your identity provider re-created your user` | `oidc_subject_holds_account` | Another OIDC subject holds the account. Usually the identity provider re-created the user. [Relink the account](#relink-an-account-after-the-identity-provider-re-created-a-user). |
| `this identity is already linked to another account: the account with this email address signs in through SAML` | `saml_subject_holds_account` | A SAML identity holds the account. An OIDC login never takes over an account of the other protocol. The user signs in through SAML. `oidc-relink` does not apply. |
| `the account with this email address is provisioned by the directory (SCIM)` | `scim_adoption_refused` | The account was created by SCIM, and this provider may not sign in SCIM-provisioned users. If this provider is the one paired with your SCIM client, turn on **Users provisioned by SCIM may sign in through this provider** (`OIDC_ADOPT_SCIM_USERS=true` for a provider configured through environment variables). `oidc-relink` does not apply. |
| `your account is linked to a different identity provider or issuer than the one you signed in with` | `provider_not_bound` | The account is bound to another provider or issuer, usually because the issuer of the provider changed. A SAML sign-in gets the same 409. If the change was on purpose, a super admin [re-binds the accounts](#rotate-an-issuer-re-bind-the-accounts). `oidc-relink` does not apply. |
| `the email address your identity provider now sends belongs to another account` | `email_held_by_another_account` | The subject is linked, and its new address is the address of a different account. Change the address of one of the two accounts. |
| `your account changed while you signed in` | `concurrent_change` | The account or the link changed during the login, for example a concurrent delete. Sign in again. |

## Relink an account after the identity provider re-created a user

When an identity provider deletes a user and creates it again, the user gets a new `sub`. The account still holds the old subject, so the login of the new subject is refused with 409:

```text
this identity is already linked to another account. If your identity provider re-created your user, ask an administrator to relink your account (elitea-main oidc-relink).
```

This refusal is intentional: only an operator can confirm that the two subjects are the same person. Do not edit `auth_core__user_provider` by hand. Use the `oidc-relink` command of the elitea-main image. It reads `DATABASE_URL`, so run it in an elitea-main pod:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Find the new subject">
    Read the new user's ID in the identity provider (in Keycloak, the user's **ID** field). Or read the `sub` value in the `OIDC: refused a login that names two accounts` warning in the elitea-main log.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Print the plan">
    ```bash
    kubectl -n <ns> exec deploy/elitea-main -- \
      /elitea-main oidc-relink --user alice@example.com --sub NEW_SUB
    ```
    `--user` takes the numeric user id or the email address. The command prints the account, its current OIDC links and the change. It writes nothing.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Apply the change">
    ```bash
    kubectl -n <ns> exec deploy/elitea-main -- \
      /elitea-main oidc-relink --user alice@example.com --sub NEW_SUB \
      --apply --operator YOUR_USER_ID
    ```
    `--operator` is your own numeric user id. It is recorded in the audit event. The user can then sign in.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Instead of `--sub`, you can use `--clear`. It removes the old link, and the next login of the user joins the account by email again. That login must pass the [sign-in rules](#oidc-sign-in-rules) for a first login, and a SCIM-provisioned account is joined only by a provider that may sign in SCIM-provisioned users.

For a SCIM-provisioned account, use `--clear`: the command refuses `--sub`. A login links such an account only through a provider that may sign in SCIM-provisioned users. An `oidc:<sub>` link does not name its provider, so the command cannot check that setting, and a link that it wrote would bypass it. After `--clear`, the next login applies the provider's own setting.

The command refuses, with exit code 2, and changes nothing when:

- the email matches more than one account (the addresses are compared without case). Give the numeric user id.
- the account has more than one OIDC link. Name the one to change with `--old-sub`.
- another account already holds the new subject. The command names that account's user id. Resolve that account first.
- `--sub` names a SCIM-provisioned account. Use `--clear`.

The command takes the same per-subject lock as a login, so a first login of the new subject and a relink to it do not both link it.

Each applied change writes the security audit event `ai.elitea.security.v1.identity.link.changed` in the same transaction. The action is `identity.oidc_link.replaced`, `identity.oidc_link.cleared` or `identity.oidc_link.added` (a new subject on an account with no OIDC link). The resource is the user, the principal is `user:<operator id>`, and the reasons are `provider_namespace:oidc` and `via:oidc-relink`. The event holds no subject and no hash of a subject: a subject is often guessable (a numeric id or an email address), so its hash would disclose it. The plan that the command prints shows the subjects to the operator. See [Audit trail](/admin/audit-trail).

The command changes OIDC links only. A SAML login with a changed NameID gets the 409 `this identity is already linked to another account`, and has no command yet.

## Session policy

### The risk

A browser session that never expires, or that stays valid while nobody uses it, extends the time an attacker can use a stolen session or an unattended computer. Your identity provider decides how often people sign in, but the platform's own session decides how long a signed-in browser keeps working.

### What is enforced

Every browser session has an absolute expiry and an idle timeout. Under **Configuration › Session Policy**, a platform administrator (permission `runtime.plugins`) sets two maximums:

| Setting | Effect | Range |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Maximum Idle Timeout (seconds)** | A session with no request for this long is ended. A session that had no idle limit gets this limit. | 0 (no maximum), or 60 to 2592000 (30 days) |
| **Maximum Session Lifetime (seconds)** | A session is ended this long after sign-in, whatever its activity. | 0 (no maximum), or 60 to 31536000 (365 days) |

Both are 0 by default, so an upgrade keeps the session lifetimes of earlier releases.

A maximum applies to new sessions when they are created, and the cookie lifetime follows it. It also applies to existing sessions: each request checks the session against the earlier of its own deadline and the deadline the maximum gives. When you lower a maximum, sessions that are already past it end at their next request. Raising a maximum never makes an existing session last longer.

Each replica of the main service reads the policy again after at most 15 seconds, and at once on the replica that saved it. If the policy cannot be read, sign-in fails with 500 and requests with a session fail with 503. The platform does not let a session through without its bound.

### Evidence

Each session has a reference number that is not secret (`browser_sessions.ref`). The platform writes one audit event for each step of a session's life:

| Action | When |
| --- | --- |
| `session.start` | A browser signed in. |
| `session.expire` | The session passed its idle timeout or its absolute lifetime. `entity_name` gives the reason, `idle` or `absolute`. |
| `session.logout` | The user signed out. |
| `session.revoke` | The server ended the session: for example, an administrator deactivated the user, or SCIM deprovisioned them. |

```json
{
  "event_type": "session",
  "action": "session.expire",
  "user_id": 4,
  "entity_type": "browser_session",
  "entity_id": 42,
  "entity_name": "provider=oidc reason=idle",
  "duration_ms": 3600000
}
```

`duration_ms` is the age of the session when it ended. A background job writes these events within about a minute, exactly once each, and an expiry carries the time the session actually ended. The events go to your SIEM with the other audit events (see [Audit evidence and retention](/admin/enterprise-controls/audit-evidence-and-retention)). No cookie or token value is ever written.

Every run that a browser session started records the session's reference (`execution_jobs.browser_session_ref`), so you can join a run to the session that started it. Runs started with an API token have no session reference.

### Customer responsibility

- Set the sign-in frequency and multi-factor policy in your identity provider. The session policy limits how long a browser stays signed in after that.
- Choose maximums that match your organisation's policy for unattended devices.

## Recent authentication

Some actions need proof that the person signed in a short time ago, not only that the browser is still signed in. A session that stays active through regular requests does not count as a recent sign-in.

### What asks for it

In this release, one action requires a recent sign-in: giving a personal access token administration scopes (see [Administration scopes on a token](#administration-scopes-on-a-token)). The check is ready for these upcoming features:

- Starting a break-glass elevation.
- Critical administrative actions.

When an action needs a recent sign-in and the session is too old, the server answers 401 with the code `reauthentication_required`. The header `X-Elitea-Reauthentication-Max-Age` gives the limit in seconds, and the header `X-Elitea-Reauthentication-Method` names the sign-in that renews the session: `oidc`, `saml` or `form`. The web app reads both and opens the matching sign-in below. An OIDC, SAML or Form browser session can satisfy the check, as described below. A personal access token, a desktop or mobile session, and a run token never do.

### The limit

Each browser session records when its person last authenticated. A request does not change that time; only a new sign-in does. With OIDC, the time is the id_token's `auth_time` claim. If the identity provider does not send `auth_time`, the time is unknown, and the session never counts as a recent sign-in. With SAML, only a sign-in that asked for `ForceAuthn` records a time: the assertion's `AuthnInstant`. An ordinary SAML sign-in records no time, so it never counts as a recent sign-in. With Form sign-in, the time is when the person last typed their password: at sign-in, or at the password prompt.

The limit is 15 minutes by default. Set `ELITEA_SESSION_REAUTHENTICATION_MAX_AGE` on the main service to change it. The value is a Go duration from `1m` to `24h`, for example `10m`. Any other value stops the main service at startup, and the error names the variable.

### Starting a sign-in with a limit

The console asks for a fresh sign-in by opening the login of the deployment's sign-in method with two parameters:

```
/auth/oidc/login?max_age=900&target_to=/admin/access
/auth/saml/login?max_age=900&target_to=/admin/access
```

- `max_age` is the limit in whole seconds, from 0 to 86400. For OIDC, the platform passes it to the identity provider. For SAML, the platform sets `ForceAuthn="true"` on the authentication request. Any other value is refused with 400.
- `target_to` is the page to return to. It must be a relative path on this site that starts with `/`. A full URL or a URL to another site is refused with 400. An ordinary sign-in would replace it with `/` instead.

The limit is saved in the signed login state (OIDC) or the signed request cookie (SAML), so it cannot be dropped from the callback.

### What the identity provider must support

For an OIDC provider:

- The provider must honour `max_age`. If the person's session at the provider is older than the limit, the provider must ask them to sign in again.
- The provider must return the `auth_time` claim in the id_token. OpenID Connect requires the claim when a request carries `max_age`.

The callback refuses the sign-in if `auth_time` is missing or older than the limit, and returns the person to the sign-in page with an error. A clock difference of up to five minutes is allowed, which is the same allowance the id_token check uses. A refused callback creates no session and does not change any existing session.

For a SAML provider:

- The provider must honour `ForceAuthn="true"`. It must ask the person to authenticate again, even when they have a session at the provider.
- The provider must send an `AuthnStatement` whose `AuthnInstant` is the time of that new authentication.

The assertion consumer service refuses the sign-in if `AuthnInstant` is missing or older than the limit, and returns the person to the sign-in page with an error. The clock skew allowed is the provider's **Clock skew** setting, the same allowance the other assertion time checks use. A refused assertion creates no session. A response to a request that did not ask for `ForceAuthn` never changes the time of any session.

Check your identity provider's documentation before you rely on the check.

### The Form password prompt

Form sign-in has no identity provider to ask. Instead, the console opens a password prompt:

```
/auth/form/reauthenticate?target_to=/admin/access
```

The prompt is a step of the Form sign-in pages. It shows the login of the current session and asks only for its password. The password is checked the same way as at sign-in, and each attempt counts against the same sign-in attempt limits. A correct password records a new authentication time, replaces the session cookie, and returns the person to `target_to`. A wrong password changes nothing and shows the prompt again. A browser without a Form session is sent to the ordinary sign-in page with the same `target_to`.

Form sessions that existed before this release have no recorded time. They count as a recent sign-in only after the person signs in again or enters the password at the prompt.

## Administration access of personal access tokens

Each personal access token records which administration permissions it may use, apart from the permissions of the person who owns it. It has one of three values:

| Value | The token may use |
|---|---|
| `none` | No administration permission. This is the value of every new token. |
| `scoped` | Only the administration permissions chosen for the token, and only while its owner still holds them. |
| `inherit` | All administration permissions of its owner, until the sunset date that a later release announces. |

The upgrade that added these values set `inherit` on each existing personal access token whose owner held an administration permission at that time. Every other existing token got `none`, and keeps `none` when its owner gets an administration role later. Desktop and mobile device sessions and project system tokens always have `none`.

The platform enforces the value. A `none` token gets `403` on every administration API, also when its owner is an administrator.

### Administration scopes on a token

A person who holds administration permissions can give a personal access token exactly the administration permissions it needs: its **administration scopes**. In the web app, choose them under **Administration access** when you create the token (see [Create a personal access token](/getting-started/create-personal-access-token)). Through the API, send `admin_access` and `admin_scopes` on `POST /api/v2/auth/token/`, or change the scopes of an existing token with `PUT /api/v2/auth/token/{uuid}/admin_scopes`:

```json
{ "admin_access": "scoped", "admin_scopes": ["access.roles.view", "llm.usage.view"] }
```

`GET /api/v2/auth/token/admin_scopes` lists the scopes you may choose. These rules apply when you set scopes:

- **From a browser sign-in only.** The request must come from an OIDC, SAML or Form browser session. A personal access token, a desktop or mobile session, an agent run token, a break-glass session, and a session under an elevation get `403`. A desktop or mobile session that may create a token at all creates a `none` token.
- **A recent sign-in.** The person must have signed in within the limit of [Recent authentication](#recent-authentication), 15 minutes by default. Otherwise the answer is `401 reauthentication_required`, and the web app sends you through a fresh sign-in and back to the form, with what you typed kept.
- **Only your own permissions.** Each scope must be an administration permission that your current sign-in holds (`403 token_scope_not_held`).
- **Never some permissions.** These permissions can never be a token scope (`403 token_scope_not_allowed`): the global administrator's own permission (`admin.auth.users.super_admin`), identity trust (`identity.sso.trust.manage`), the four break-glass permissions (`access.breakglass.*`), and the two legacy names `runtime.plugins` and `configuration.governance`. Choose the narrow permissions that replace the legacy names instead.
- **Never `inherit`.** `admin_access` takes `none` or `scoped`. No request sets `inherit`.

Removing every scope (`{"admin_access": "none"}`) needs a browser sign-in but no recent one. Use it, or a `scoped` change, to move an `inherit` token off `inherit` before its sunset date. Each change of a token's scopes writes the security audit event `admin.access.changed` with the kind `token_scopes`. Refusals are recorded in the admin audit trail with the action `token.mint_refused` (create) or `token.admin_scopes_refused` (change).

A scoped token still follows its owner: a scope that the owner loses stops working at the next request. And it still follows the [critical action rule](#tokens-and-critical-actions) below.

The token responses carry `admin_access` and `admin_scopes`. An `inherit` token also carries `inherit_ends_on`, the date it ends, once the sunset date is set: the later of the sunset date and 30 days after its owner's notice was delivered, and the sunset date until then. A holder of `access.roles.view` lists every token with `scoped` or `inherit` access, with its owner, at `GET /api/v2/admin/access/tokens` (paged with `limit`, at most 200, and `offset`). The listing never shows a token value.

Once the sunset date is set, the personal access token expiry routine (the scheduled sweep, or `POST /api/v2/admin/background_jobs/administration/pat_expiry_notices:run`, whose answer adds `inherit_owners` and `inherit_queued`) queues one e-mail per owner of an `inherit` token, listing that owner's `inherit` tokens and the end date. The `main.access_notice_sender` routine sends it like the [break-glass notices](/admin/break-glass#who-is-told) and records the delivery time on each listed token (`elitea_identity.token_lifecycle.inherit_notice_delivered_at`). A send that fails records nothing and is retried, and the owner stays listed until a delivery is recorded. A suspended owner, an owner with no e-mail address and an expired token are left out. A deployment that upgrades after the sunset date was set sends the notice when it first runs.

### Tokens and critical actions

Some administration actions are **critical**: changing who is an administrator, editing the permissions of a role, deleting a role or a project, the identity provider and SCIM trust actions, releasing a legal hold, shortening retention, and the LLM provider routes. A personal access token with `scoped` or `inherit` access gets `403` with the code `token_critical_action_refused` on a critical action. Sign in to the console to do it. The same token keeps working on every other administration action its access covers.

One exception keeps a broken sign-in repairable: the identity provider and SCIM trust actions (create, change the trust settings of, turn off, delete or re-bind an identity provider; create or rotate a SCIM client) accept an `inherit` token whose owner is a global administrator by their standing role. A `scoped` token, or an `inherit` token of any other administrator, is refused them. The exception ends at the sunset date of `inherit`.

On three routes the danger depends on what the request changes, and only the critical change is refused to a token:

- **Gateway governance rows**: creating, changing or deleting a guardrail is refused. A change that turns a guardrail into another kind of row, or another row into a guardrail, counts as a guardrail change. Routing rules, throttles and the other kinds of rows stay open.
- **Global provider update**: a change of the endpoint, the credential, the keyless identity, the type or any field that is not a setting is refused. A change of only the label, the display name, the description or the tags is allowed. The update is compared with the stored provider, so a body that repeats the stored endpoint unchanged is still a settings change. If the provider changes while a token's settings update is in flight, the update gets `409`: read the provider again and retry.
- **Retention**: a change that lets data be deleted sooner is refused. That covers a lower maximum, a maximum where data was kept forever, a lower minimum, a project override below what applies now, and turning on project overrides for a class (a stored override below the platform maximum then takes effect). A change that keeps data as long or longer is allowed, and so is deleting an override: an override never applies above the platform maximum, so the project falls back to as long or longer.

Every refusal is recorded in the admin audit trail with the action `admin.token_refused`. The upgrade note lists every critical action.

## Which credential carries which administration rights

The administration permissions of a request come from the credential that sent it, not only from the person who owns it:

| Credential | Administration permissions |
|---|---|
| Browser session (OIDC, SAML or Form sign-in) | All of the owner's. |
| Personal access token | By its value above: none, its scopes that the owner still holds, or all of the owner's. |
| Desktop or mobile device session | Only the two checks the apps make in place: working during a maintenance window, and Marketplace moderation. No administration API answers it. |
| Agent run token | None. |
| Project system token | None. |

A request with the `X-API-Key` header follows the credential the header carries: a personal access token or a run token. A `scoped` token is checked on each request, so a permission its owner loses is lost by the token at once. Project and developer permissions do not depend on the credential.

## Connect an identity provider with SCIM

SCIM lets an identity provider push its directory. It creates accounts when people join and deactivates them when they leave. The SCIM endpoint is `/api/v2/scim/v2`.

An identity provider authenticates to SCIM with a **SCIM client**. A SCIM client is a credential that belongs to the integration, not to a person. The audit trail records its changes as `scim:<client name>`.

<Warning>
**Breaking change.** SCIM no longer accepts personal access tokens or browser sessions. A request with one gets `401` and the detail "SCIM requires a SCIM client credential; personal access tokens are not accepted". If your identity provider used a personal access token, create a SCIM client and replace the token in the identity provider.
</Warning>

<Steps>
  <Step title="Open Configuration › Authentication › SCIM provisioning">
    The panel shows the **Tenant URL** and the **Token endpoint** of this deployment, each with a copy button. You need `identity.sso.view` or `admin.auth.users` to see the SCIM clients, and `identity.sso.settings.manage` or `admin.auth.users` to revoke one (see [Who can change what](#who-can-change-what)). Only a super admin can create, rotate or delete a SCIM client, because a SCIM client can create and change accounts.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Create a SCIM client">
    Select **New SCIM client**. Type a name for the identity provider, and select a method:

    - **Secret token (Bearer)** — one secret. The identity provider sends it as the bearer token on each request.
    - **OAuth2 client credentials** — a client identifier and a client secret. The identity provider gets a short-lived access token from the token endpoint (1 hour by default), and sends that access token.

    Optionally, set **Expires on**. The date must be from tomorrow to two years ahead. After that day the client stops working. The list shows the days left, and it shows a warning in the last 30 days.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Copy the secret">
    The secret shows **one time only**. Copy it into the identity provider before you close the dialog. Elitea keeps only a hash of the secret, so nobody can show it again.
  </Step>
</Steps>

### Microsoft Entra ID settings

In the Entra ID enterprise application, open **Provisioning**, set the mode to **Automatic**, and use these values:

| Entra ID field | Bearer authentication | OAuth2 client credentials grant |
|---|---|---|
| Authentication method | Bearer authentication | OAuth2 client credentials grant |
| Tenant URL | `https://<host>/api/v2/scim/v2` | `https://<host>/api/v2/scim/v2` |
| Secret token | the `scim_…` secret | — |
| Token endpoint | — | `https://<host>/api/v2/scim/oauth/token` |
| Client identifier | — | the `scimc_…` client identifier |
| Client secret | — | the `scimcs_…` client secret |

Select **Test connection**. Entra ID calls `/Users` with the credential, and a correct credential answers `200`.

The token endpoint accepts `grant_type=client_credentials` with the client identifier and secret in the form body (`client_secret_post`) or in an HTTP Basic header (`client_secret_basic`). The operator sets the access token lifetime with `ELITEA_SCIM_ACCESS_TOKEN_TTL` (between `5m` and `24h`).

<Note>
Maintenance mode does not stop SCIM. A SCIM client has no administration permissions, and the maintenance gate admits only administrators, so the gate does not apply to SCIM. The identity provider continues to create, update and deactivate accounts during a maintenance window. To freeze the directory, pause provisioning in the identity provider.
</Note>

Each OAuth2 client can hold at most 20 live access tokens. A new token removes the oldest one. Failed token requests are limited per client identifier and caller address. After 20 failures in one minute, that address gets `429` until the minute ends, also with the correct secret. Failures from one address never block another address, so an attacker cannot lock out your identity provider. To know the real caller address behind an ingress, set the proxy networks in `ELITEA_TRUSTED_PROXY_CIDRS` (or `trusted_proxy_cidrs` in the Form authentication document). Without them the caller address is unknown. The endpoint then checks every secret first, so a correct secret is never refused, and it limits only failures.

### Rotate or revoke a SCIM client

- **Rotate** makes a new secret. The old secret stops working immediately, and every access token issued with it stops working too. Put the new secret in the identity provider at once.
- **Revoke** stops the client from authenticating. The row stays in the list, so the audit trail can still name it.
- **Delete** removes the client.

## Bind SCIM groups to a project role

What the admin console does provide is the group-to-role mapping, beneath the identity provider list:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Create a binding">
    Name the SCIM group exactly as your identity provider sends it, and choose the project and role it grants.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Understand membership">
    A member the group granted the role to loses it when they leave the group. A member who already held that role independently keeps it — the identity provider is authoritative only over what it granted, not over everything it happens to find.
  </Step>

<Note>
A SCIM group with no binding is refused by name; it never creates a project on its own, and it never guesses a role.
</Note>

## What SCIM may and may not change

SCIM manages ordinary accounts: it creates them, renames them, changes their display name and name parts, and deactivates them. A SCIM `DELETE` deactivates (suspends) the account instead of deleting it, so the person's work stays attached if they return.

Some accounts are off limits to SCIM. Any write to them is refused with `403` (`scimType: mutability`), the reason is included in the response, and the refused attempt is recorded in the **Audit Trail**:

- **Accounts with an administration role** (any role on the admin console's Users page). SCIM cannot rename, suspend or deprovision them, including people who became administrators after SCIM created their account. This stops a SCIM credential from taking over or locking out the people who administer the platform.
- **Platform accounts** — addresses in the `@centry.user` domain, such as `system@centry.user` and the per-project `system_user_<n>@centry.user`. SCIM also cannot give any account an address in that domain, and a SCIM group cannot grant a project role to a platform account.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Deprovision an administrator">
    When your identity provider tries to deprovision someone who holds an administration role, the request is refused with "SCIM deprovisioning refused: account holds an administration role — remove the role, then let the IdP retry". Until then, the person's sessions and access tokens keep working.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Remove the role">
    On the admin console's **Users** page, remove the person's administration role. You can also suspend them from that page right away.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Let the identity provider retry">
    The next provisioning cycle repeats the request, and this time it succeeds.
  </Step>
</Steps>

### Deprovision the last administrator

The [last administrator](/admin/users-and-roles#the-last-administrator) of the deployment is never deactivated, by SCIM or anyone else: the deployment would have nobody left to administer it. But when your identity provider deactivates that person (`PATCH` with `active: false`, `PUT` with `active: false`, or `DELETE`), the organisation has said that the person left, so the access ends anyway:

- the account stays active and keeps its roles, so the deployment keeps an administrator on record;
- the person's browser sessions, device sessions and personal access tokens are revoked at once: the next request with any of them is refused;
- one `critical` security audit event is written (action `admin.access.deprovision_refused`), and the Audit Trail records `role.last_administrator_refused`. A retry that finds no session or token left to end writes no second critical event; one that ends a new sign-in does;
- the identity provider gets a SCIM error with status `409` that says to give a successor the role.

Then give someone else the role: from the console of another administrator, or, when there is none, with [`elitea-main admin-role grant`](/deployment/operations#administrative-roles-from-the-command-line). The next provisioning cycle repeats the deactivation. While a second administrator exists, it is refused as for any administrator (above): remove the person's role, and the cycle after that deactivates the account.

<Note>
SCIM stores one address per account, and that address is the SCIM `userName`. A change to the work email alone is accepted but does not change the account's address. Only a `userName` change does.
</Note>
