Break-glass access gives a person the administration permissions of the `admin` or `super_admin` role for a short time, in one browser session, with a stated reason. An elevation is not a role: it is bound to the session that asked for it, it ends by itself, and every elevation is reviewed after it ends.

<Note>
This release ships the policy, the ledger and its housekeeping. No route starts an elevation yet: the request, approve, start and end routes come in a later release. The settings below are stored now and become the policy of those routes when they ship.
</Note>

## Settings

The **Break-glass** section of **Configuration** holds the policy. Only a holder of `access.breakglass.manage` sees it or changes it. Changing it is a `critical` action: it is refused under an elevation and refused to every personal access token.

| Setting | Default | Range |
|---|---|---|
| Approval required | on | on or off (see below) |
| Online default duration | 60 minutes | 1 to the online cap |
| Online maximum duration (the cap) | 240 minutes | 1 to **480** (the ceiling in code) |
| Sealed default duration | 60 minutes | 1 to the sealed cap |
| Sealed maximum duration (the cap) | 120 minutes | 1 to **240** (the ceiling in code) |
| Request lifetime | 30 minutes | 1 to 1440 |
| Review time, online | 72 hours | 1 to 720 |
| Review time after a sealed sign-in | 24 hours | 1 to 720 |
| Re-authentication window for the start | 15 minutes | 1 to 15 |
| Approval-off delay | 24 hours | 1 to 168 |

A deployment can lower any value. It can raise a cap only up to the ceiling in code: 480 minutes online and 240 sealed. A save above a ceiling, or a default above its cap, is refused with `400` and names the setting.

### Switching approval off

Dual control is the default: a second person with `access.breakglass.approve` approves every request. Switching approval off:

- needs `admin.auth.users.super_admin` from a standing role. A holder of the section without it gets `403`, and still edits the durations. An elevation never counts towards it;
- takes effect after the **approval-off delay** that was in force before the save (24 hours by default), so a save that also lowers the delay does not shorten its own wait. Until then the section shows the state `off_scheduled`, and a request still needs an approver;
- writes a security audit event at once: `admin.access.break_glass_approval` with the reason code `approval_off_requested` (see [the audit event schema](/reference/audit-event-schema)).

Shortening the approval-off delay needs the same standing `super_admin` (`403` otherwise), since a shorter delay weakens the next switch-off as much as the switch-off itself. Lengthening it needs only the section.

Switching approval back on needs only the section, takes effect at once, cancels a pending switch-off, and writes the same event with `approval_on_restored`.

## When an elevation or a request runs out

Access stops by itself. An elevation grants nothing once its expiry passes, whether or not anything has run since: the permission check compares the expiry with the clock.

The record catches up on its own. The `main.elevation_reconciler` routine runs every minute (see [Schedules and tasks](/admin/schedules-and-tasks)) and, for each row, in one transaction:

- an elevation past its expiry that nobody ended is recorded as ended with the end reason `expired`, gets its `expired` security audit event, and moves to `review_pending`;
- a request that nobody approved or denied within the **request lifetime** is recorded as `request_expired` and gets its `request_expired` event. An approval of it afterwards is refused. The lifetime is the one stored when the routine runs, so lowering it also expires the pending requests older than the new value.

An elevation that ends early (by its holder, an approver or the operator command) moves to `review_pending` at the same moment, with its `ended` event. If the audit event cannot be written, the row is left as it was and the next pass completes it; access is not affected either way. One replica runs each pass, and two passes never record the same row twice.

To end an elevation without the console, see the [break-glass runbook](/deployment/break-glass-runbook).

## What an elevation cannot do

An elevation must not make itself permanent or switch off the controls that watch it. Every administration action carries an elevation value in the platform's action table, and the platform applies it when the permission that admitted the request came from the elevation:

- **allow** (most actions): the elevation may do it.
- **flag**: allowed, and the audit row of the request is marked `elevation_flagged` for the elevation review (see [Role use on administration rows](/admin/audit-trail#role-use-on-administration-rows)).
- **deny**: refused with `403` and the code `elevation_restricted`, at either level.
- **sealed**: the identity trust actions. Refused with `403 elevation_restricted` under an elevation a person approved, or one that started with approval off, at either level, and under a sealed elevation at the `admin` level. Allowed and flagged only under a sealed elevation at the `super_admin` level: repairing an identity provider while nobody can sign in is what the sealed path is for.

The value belongs to the action, not to the permission: shortening a retention policy is refused, lengthening it with the same permission is not. A person whose **standing** roles already admit an action is not restricted by these values, elevated or not. An elevation also creates no personal access token (`403 elevation_restricted` on **Create token**; a request with admin scopes gets the scope rules' own codes `token_scopes_elevated` or `token_scopes_sealed_session` first), and a sealed session never does.

## Sealed sessions

A sealed session is how a [sealed credential](/deployment/break-glass-runbook#sealed-credentials) signs in when single sign-on is down. It is not an ordinary session with more permissions. It is a separate kind of session with its own limits:

- **Its own cookie.** It is carried by the cookie `__Host-elitea_sealed`, which is `Secure`, `HttpOnly` and `SameSite=Strict`, with the path `/` and no domain. It never replaces the person's ordinary session cookie, and neither cookie can stand in for the other.
- **The administration surface only.** The cookie counts only on routes of the administration surface: the administration console and the administration routes of the API. On every other route the platform ignores it, as if the browser had not sent it. A sealed session cannot open chat, agents or any project content, and it holds nothing in the developer and default modes.
- **The elevation only.** A sealed session holds the permissions of its elevation's level (`admin` or `super_admin`, the level of the credential) and nothing else. The person's standing roles add nothing: a person who is `super_admin` signs in at the `admin` level with an `admin` credential and gets only the `admin` set.
- **No identity provider.** A sealed session never refreshes, introspects or calls an identity provider. It is checked against the database only, so it works while the provider is down.
- **It ends with its elevation.** Its expiry is the elevation's expiry: the sealed duration of the settings above, and never more than 240 minutes, the ceiling in code. It also ends at once when the elevation ends early: by its holder, an approver, the operator command `break-glass end` (see the [runbook](/deployment/break-glass-runbook#end-an-elevation)), or a session or account event. From the next request on, the cookie counts as absent: the browser is told to drop it, and the request is unauthenticated unless the browser also holds an ordinary session, which then decides with the person's standing roles only.
- **The idle limit still applies.** The maximum idle time of the session policy, when one is set, applies to a sealed session too: one left unused that long is signed out before its elevation runs out. Its elevation then grants nothing (no session can use it) but stays open in the ledger until its expiry, or until `break-glass end` or the console ends it.
- **No recent-authentication proof.** An action that asks for a recent sign-in refuses a sealed session, and so does every `critical` action, which is refused under any elevation.

A sealed session needs HTTPS. Browsers accept a `__Host-` cookie only from a secure origin, so on a deployment served over plain HTTP (a local development stack, for example) the sealed path does not work. The platform does not weaken the cookie to make it work there.

<Note>
This release ships the session kind, its cookie and its limits. The sign-in page that turns a sealed credential into a sealed session comes in a later release.
</Note>
