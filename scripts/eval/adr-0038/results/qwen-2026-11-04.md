## Analysis

Total tokens per run (prompt plus completion): (a) 24.6k, (b) 82.7k,
(c) 27.4k, (d) 57.1k, (e) 55.2k.

### Does `auto` keep the quality of (a) at the cost of (b)?

Yes, and on this model it does better than both.

- Quality: `auto` passed 88 % of runs. Thinking on passed 83 %. Thinking
  off passed 77 %. On coding the order is the same: 86 %, 71 %, 68 %.
- Reasoning tokens: `auto` used 1.0k per run, 80 % fewer than thinking on
  (5.1k).
- Wall time: `auto` took 75 s per run. Thinking on took 219 s. Thinking off
  took 91 s.
- Thinking off is not cheap on coding. It needs about twice the steps
  (17.4 against 8.4 calls), and every step sends the transcript again. Its
  prompt tokens are 3.8 times those of `auto` (139k against 37k). Nine of
  its runs stopped at the 40-step limit while they still tried fixes.
- Thinking on loses through the output cap. Nine of its 52 runs stopped at
  12,288 completion tokens in one step: long reasoning plus a whole-file
  `write_file` in the same reply. A larger cap can help (a), but the
  product also has caps. This is the truncation risk that ADR-0038
  decision 8 names.

### Does the loop beat the single run, and at what cost?

Yes on quality. The cost is about two times the tokens and five to six
times the wall time of a single `auto` run.

- Quality: the loop passed 98 % of runs. The best single variant (`auto`)
  passed 88 %. On coding the loop passed 28 of 28 runs, against 24 of 28.
- Cost against (c): prompt tokens times 1.8, answer tokens times 2,
  reasoning tokens times 7.7 (mostly the manager: 5.9k per run), wall time
  times 5.7 (424 s against 75 s).
- The gain does not come from many rounds. The mean was 1.2 rounds, and 45
  of 52 runs ended after round 1.
- The gain also does not come from telling the agent the check. The control
  (e) gives a single `auto` run the same check description. It passed 85 %
  (coding 86 %), the same as (c), at twice the prompt tokens of (c).
- What remains is the plan step. The manager thinks over the baseline test
  output, writes the acceptance criteria and gives one focused task. The
  worker then has a 20-step limit and a fresh context. In two coding runs
  the worker hit the step limit, the summary call kept its work, and the
  next round passed.

### Where did the loop get worse?

- **A schema pass is not a content pass.** In `extract-lease` (repeat 1)
  the output was valid against the schema, so the loop ended "verified".
  The end date was wrong. The `schema` verifier is strong only for shape.
  A "verified" badge on such a run says too much.
- **Overfitting to the public tests.** In `code-template-sections` the loop
  ended "verified" in both repeats, and both times the held-out tests
  failed. On the held-out tests the loop passed 8 of 10, the same as
  thinking on. A passing public check does not prove the held-out
  behaviour.
- **The reviewer adds rounds without a measured gain.** On the doc tasks the
  reviewer rejected first drafts that already passed the rubric. The loop
  used 1.9 rounds, 13k reasoning tokens and 595 s per run, against 36 s for
  (c). All variants passed every doc rubric, so the evaluation cannot show
  a gain.
- **The hard rules never fired.** There were no `done` overrides, no
  regression restores, no flaky confirms and no same-task stops. The tasks
  did not push the loop into those paths. The unit tests in `test/` cover
  them.

### Recommendations

- **Qwen default: `auto`.** It gives the best quality, the fewest reasoning
  tokens and the shortest wall time of the three policies. Keep thinking
  off for summary, finish and naming calls.
- **Verified loop: go, as an opt-in mode.** It raised coding from 86 % to
  100 % on this set. It costs about twice the tokens and five times the
  wall time, so it must not be the default.
- Label a run that only a `schema` verifier checked as "shape verified" or
  similar, not plain "verified".
- Do not offer the `review` verifier as an agent default yet. This
  evaluation has no planted wrong results, so its reject rate is unknown
  (ADR-0038 Verification section).
- Before the decision on other providers, add harder doc tasks and planted
  wrong results.

### Limits

- **Small sample.** 26 tasks and 2 repeats. Between repeats, 20 to 25 of 26
  tasks gave the same result per variant, even at temperature 0. A
  difference of two or three runs is in the noise. The coding gain of the
  loop (4 of 28 runs against (c)) is larger than that noise, but it is not
  large.
- **Ceilings.** All variants passed all doc tasks. Extraction was close to
  the top (75 % to 94 %). Only the coding group separates the variants
  well.
- **Load.** Up to 8 runs shared one vLLM server. Wall times include
  queueing. The variants ran interleaved, so the ratios are fair. Variant
  (e) ran later, without the loop runs beside it.
- **Output cap.** The 12,288-token cap per call shaped the result of (a).
- **Prompt cost.** The token counts are nominal. vLLM prefix caching makes
  resent prompts cheaper than the counts show. This favours (b) and the
  loop workers.
- **Restarted runs.** Two loop runs first failed on a client timeout (fetch
  drops a response after 300 s). After the fix they ran again. The results
  keep the second attempt.
- **One model.** The `gpt`, `claude` and `gemini` entries in
  `providers.json` are templates. Nobody has run them yet.
